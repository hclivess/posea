\begin{abstract}
Permissionless systems that distribute rewards per participant rather than per unit of
capital face an adversary that ordinary countermeasures cannot price: the identity farm.
We observe that every per-identity admission rule --- registration work, waiting periods,
per-address caps, per-subnet budgets, probation --- imposes a cost that is linear in the
number of identities, and therefore charges an honest participant holding one identity
exactly what it charges an adversary holding $n$, per identity. The adversary amortises;
the honest participant does not. We report a deployment in which this failure was total:
on a live chain, approximately $1{,}000$ of $1{,}191$ registered mining identities belonged
to two or three operators running browser farms on rented servers, capturing roughly $42\%$
of block emission.

We describe Proof of Secure Element Attestation (PoSEA), which replaces the attempt to make
identities \emph{expensive} with an attempt to make them \emph{scarce}. Admission requires a
WebAuthn hardware attestation: the device generates a non-exportable key inside its secure
element and signs a chain-selected challenge, producing a certificate chain terminating at a
vendor attestation root pinned as a consensus constant. Verification is deterministic and
offline; no validator contacts a vendor. Where the device class exposes a per-device
certificate, that certificate is bound to a single identity for the duration of a lease.
Block production is unchanged and remains a deterministic beacon-keyed weighted draw, so the
mechanism confers no advantage on faster hardware.

We give the deployment results, and we state plainly the two costs: the scheme places vendor
attestation authorities in the trust path, and it excludes devices whose owners have unlocked
them. We identify the security of the construction as reducing to an unresolved empirical
question --- whether the cost of extracting attestation keys from a secure element is
per-model or generalises across a chip family --- and we invite work on it.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A permissionless network that wishes to reward people rather than capital must decide who
counts as a person. Proof of Work answers by making participation expensive in energy; Proof
of Stake answers by making it expensive in capital. Both are auctions, and in an auction the
participant with the most of the scarce input wins. Concentration follows by construction
rather than by accident.

Systems that attempt to escape this by paying per participant inherit a different problem.
If an identity can be minted by a script, the reward per identity becomes a subsidy to
whoever can mint the most.

This is not a hypothetical failure. NANO's representative spam, Idena's validation
ceremonies and Nyzo's cycle each encountered it, and each responded with additional
per-identity rules that the next operator circumvented by the same method as the last. We
report a further instance, with measurements, and argue that the recurrence is structural.

\subsection{Deployment context}

NADO is a permissionless chain with a two-lane block production schedule. A \emph{bonded}
lane weights producers by stake. An \emph{open} lane, which receives $30\%$ of slots, was
designed so that a participant with no capital could produce blocks from an ordinary mobile
device. Selection in both lanes is a deterministic, beacon-keyed weighted draw: exactly one
hash determines each slot's producer, so there is no grinding advantage and no hash race.

The open lane was defended, successively, by a non-parallelisable sequential work proof at
registration, a per-subnet ingress budget, a per-address identity cap, and a probationary
period before a new identity could earn. Each was deployed in response to observed farming.
Each failed.

\begin{observation}[Linearity]
\label{obs:linearity}
Let $c$ be the cost imposed by an admission rule on a single identity, and let an adversary
control $n$ identities. The adversary's total cost is $nc$ and its reward is $n r$ for
per-identity reward $r$; the honest participant's cost is $c$ against reward $r$. The ratio
of cost to reward is $c/r$ for both. Raising $c$ therefore does not disadvantage the
adversary relative to the honest participant. It disadvantages every participant equally,
and prices out the honest participant first, because the adversary amortises fixed setup
across $n$ while the honest participant does not.
\end{observation}

Observation~\ref{obs:linearity} is elementary, and we state it explicitly because the
literature and practice both continue to propose per-identity rules as anti-Sybil measures.
The rule does not have to be weak to fail. It fails because it is per identity.

\subsection{Measurement}

On 2026-09-06 the open lane carried $1{,}191$ registered identities. Approximately $1{,}000$
of these were attributable to two or three operators running headless browser farms on
rented Linux and Windows servers. The farmed set captured approximately $42\%$ of total
emission. Every honest participant was, in effect, subsidising three people.

\section{Construction}

\subsection{Intuition}

Only two properties resist replication by an adversary with money but no people: capital,
which the bonded lane already weighs, and a physically distinct object. PoSEA anchors open-lane
identity in the second.

A secure element is a physically distinct object that can attest to its own genuineness. It
cannot be instantiated in software, and --- crucially --- it cannot be instantiated in
software \emph{by an adversary who already controls the host}.

\begin{definition}[PoSEA admission]
An identity is admitted to the open lane for a lease period if it presents a WebAuthn
attestation statement in which
\begin{enumerate}
  \item a non-exportable key was generated inside a secure element,
  \item that key signs a challenge derived from a chain-selected beacon value,
  \item the attestation certificate chain terminates at a vendor root pinned in the protocol,
  \item the attested boot state indicates a locked bootloader and verified boot, and
  \item where the device class exposes a per-device certificate, that certificate is not
        currently bound to another identity.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

\subsection{Device classes}

Four classes expose hardware attestation to a web page. They differ in whether they yield a
per-device handle, which determines whether condition (5) is enforceable.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}llll@{}}
\toprule
Class & Format & Pinned root & Per-device? \\
\midrule
Android, locked bootloader & \texttt{android-key} & Google HW attestation roots & Yes (RKP, Android 12+) \\
Windows, physical TPM 2.0 & \texttt{tpm} & Microsoft TPM Root CA 2014 & Yes (AIK per TPM/account) \\
FIDO2 security key & \texttt{packed} & per-AAGUID FIDO MDS roots & No (batch, $\geq 10^5$ units) \\
Apple Secure Enclave & \texttt{apple} & Apple WebAuthn Root CA & No attestation via passkeys \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

On Android with remote key provisioning, the attestation certificate at position
\texttt{x5c[1]} is unique to the device and reused for every credential it creates until
rotation (approximately two weeks in the observed sample). On Windows, the AIK certificate is
unique to the pair (TPM, account). These two classes therefore admit enforcement of condition
(5), and are the classes the deployment accepts.

FIDO2 authenticators issue one batch certificate per $\geq 10^5$ units by design, as a privacy
measure; they identify a model, not a unit, and are refused. Apple passkeys return
\texttt{fmt: none} and carry no attestation at all (iOS 16+, macOS 13+), confirmed on
2026-09-07, so Apple devices cannot attest through a web page. A native App Attest bridge is
the only available route and is not deployed.

\subsection{Roots as consensus constants}

Vendor roots are pinned by SHA-256 over DER and stored with the protocol. They are never
fetched, never learned from a peer, and change only by a gated protocol commit. The FIDO
metadata snapshot maps each AAGUID to its own roots, so an authenticator from one vendor
cannot claim another's identity.

Revocation lists are deliberately not consulted at validation time, because doing so would
make validation non-deterministic and network-dependent. A compromised intermediate is
handled as a root change, by commit. This is a conscious trade of revocation latency for
deterministic replay.

\subsection{What the mechanism does and does not prove}

It proves that the registration was produced on genuine hardware of a stated class, by a key
resident in that hardware, over a challenge the chain chose and could not be precomputed; and,
on renewal, that the same device key was present at a later epoch.

It does not prove personhood. A single human with a genuine locked device may create as many
identities as they physically tap, since WebAuthn requires user activation per signature. The
security claim is therefore that the cost of an identity is \emph{non-automatable human work
on genuine hardware}, not that identities are one-to-one with people. Rooting is not a
loophole: a rooted device is precisely what would automate the tap, and a rooted device
cannot attest, because Android key attestation reports boot state from the secure element and
the verifier requires \texttt{deviceLocked} and \texttt{verifiedBootState == Verified}.

\subsection{What is unchanged}

PoSEA is an admission mechanism and nothing else. Block production remains the deterministic
beacon-keyed weighted draw; attestation decides membership of the draw, not its outcome, so
faster hardware confers no advantage. Finality remains a bonded-stake quorum with
Casper-style epoch checkpoints. Attestation and presence weight are deliberately excluded
from fork-choice weight and from the finality quorum: presence earns reward, and never
purchases a say in what is final.

\section{Deployment}

The mechanism was deployed as a genesis reroll with balances carried forward. It permitted the
retirement of the entire per-identity apparatus that had preceded it: the sequential work
proof and its difficulty machinery, the per-subnet ingress budget, the per-address identity
cap, and probation. An attested identity now earns from its first lease.

The lease is $360$ epochs at six-minute epochs, or $36$ hours. Android and TPM bindings are
\emph{leased} rather than permanent because their attestation keys rotate; a binding that
outlived the key would permit one device to bind a fresh identity per rotation. Renewal
re-attests.

\section{Costs, stated plainly}

\subsection{Vendor authority in the trust path}

Verification requires trusting that Google's and Microsoft's attestation roots sign only
genuine hardware. This is a centralisation cost in a system whose premise is that no such
authority should be necessary, and we do not think it can be argued away.

We observe only that every Sybil mechanism trusts something: Proof of Work trusts that no
coalition controls a hash majority, an assumption weakening empirically; Proof of Stake trusts
that concentrated holders do not collude. PoSEA trusts pinned vendor roots, verified offline,
with no service able to revoke a participant at validation time. We regard this as a different
trust assumption rather than a strictly worse one, and we note that reasonable readers
disagree.

\subsection{Exclusion of modified devices}

A device whose owner has unlocked its bootloader cannot attest and cannot mine in the open
lane. This excludes, disproportionately, the users most committed to the principle that an
owner should control their own hardware --- that is, the constituency most sympathetic to a
permissionless chain. The deployment mitigates this only partially, by a bonded lane requiring
no device at all, which is a capital gate and therefore not a substitute.

\section{Open problem}

The security of the construction reduces to an empirical quantity we cannot presently bound:
the cost of extracting an attestation private key from a secure element, and whether that cost
is specific to a hardware model or generalises across a chip family.

If extraction is per-model and expensive, device scarcity holds and the adversary must
purchase devices. If a single extraction generalises across a family, the adversary obtains an
oracle for minting attestations and the construction degrades to precisely the identity farm it
was built to prevent, with the additional disadvantage of having excluded honest modified
devices in the meantime.

We are not aware of a satisfying public treatment of this question at the granularity the
design requires, and we regard establishing it as the most valuable next step. We would rather
learn that the assumption fails than deploy on it silently.

\section{Conclusion}

Per-identity admission rules cannot solve identity farming, because their cost is linear in
identities and therefore neutral between an honest participant and an adversary. The
alternative is to change what an identity is. Anchoring it in a physically distinct secure
element makes identity scarce rather than expensive, at the price of admitting vendor
attestation authorities into the trust path and excluding owners who have taken control of
their hardware. Whether that price is worth paying depends on an empirical question about
secure element extraction costs that remains, to our knowledge, open.
