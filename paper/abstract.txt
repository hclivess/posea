A TPM cannot prove which chip a key lives in without help. Its endorsement key is vendor-certified but is, by specification, a restricted decryption key that cannot sign; the signing key that produces an attestation must therefore be certified by someone, and in practice that someone is a privacy certificate authority holding a key able to assert any chip's identity. We measured what happens when that authority is unavailable: across 664 device-attestation attempts on a public deployment, 26.8% came from machines with healthy TPM~2.0 hardware whose platform attestation service has no authority registered for the chip and answers HTTP~404. On Linux the service does not exist and never has. We show the authority can be removed entirely. The construction rests on a property of the TPM command itself: TPM2_MakeCredential's credential blob is a deterministic function of its (seed, object name, secret) triple, so a challenge issued once can be recomputed offline by every verifier afterwards, indefinitely. An inherently interactive possession proof therefore does not need to be signed into an artifact—it needs only to be ordered, and a ledger, log, or any service supplying an agreed total order provides that for free. Four published messages, with a commit--reveal across k challengers drawn by a public beacon, replace the issuer. Nothing is signed and no key persists. We give the protocol, show its security reduces to publication order and to one TPM object attribute, and analyse what key extraction actually buys against it: one identity per chip physically held, however far the technique generalises, since certifying an uncertified key still needs the vendor. We validate against a TPM~2.0 simulator and a physical AMD firmware TPM, and report deployment as an admission mechanism on a public chain, where the residual assumption is vendor certificate authority integrity and nothing else.
